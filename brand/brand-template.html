Microservices Governance Framework

A construction model for building microservices from domain to operations
Author Jesús Ariel González Bonilla Version 1.0.0 Date August 2026 License MIT License

Overview

The Microservices Governance Framework (MGF) is a structured guide for teams building distributed systems. It organizes twenty numbered sections with explicit dependency rules — from understanding the business domain to operating services in production.

Unlike a collection of articles, the MGF is a construction model: a sequence of decisions, artefacts, and governance rules a team follows to build a software system in an ordered, traceable, and reproducible way.

Key principle
Ideas are not protected; their original selection, arrangement, and expression are. The MGF does not own DDD, C4, or hexagonal architecture — it owns the orchestration.

Section dependency model

Each section depends on its predecessors. Architecture cannot be written before the domain is mapped; API contracts cannot be specified before the architecture is defined.

domain → product → requirements
             ↓
        architecture → api → quality
             ↓
         operations ← devops

Article · Software Architecture

Why Governance Matters Before the First Line of Code

The hidden cost of teams that build without a shared model of what "done" means
Author Jesús Ariel González Bonilla Published August 2026 Reading time 8 min License CC BY 4.0

The problem no one names

Most software teams fail not because of bad code, but because of bad agreement. They disagree — silently — on what a bounded context is, on whether a service needs a runbook, on who decides when an API contract is ready. They discover the disagreement in production.

Governance is the word we use when we mean: the explicit agreement about how we build, in what order, with what evidence that we are done. It is not bureaucracy. It is the shared model that makes speed possible.

The cost of implicit models

Every team has a model. The question is whether it is written down or lives only in the heads of the three people who were there at the beginning.

"A system without explicit governance is governed by whoever writes the next commit."

When the implicit model breaks — and it always breaks when the team grows or when those three people leave — the reconstruction cost is almost always higher than the original investment in documentation would have been.

Important
Governance is not documentation for its own sake. It is the minimum set of shared decisions that lets the team move independently without constant synchronisation overhead.

What an explicit model buys you

  • New engineers reach production-ready in days, not months
  • Architecture decisions are traceable — you can answer why
  • Quality gates are known before the sprint starts

Book · Chapter 1

The Architecture of Knowledge

How software teams encode what they know — and what happens when they don't
Author Jesús Ariel González Bonilla Series Software Without Borders License CC BY 4.0

Chapter 1 · The invisible architecture

Before there is a system, there is a decision. Before the decision, there is a model — usually unspoken, usually incomplete, always consequential. This book is about making that model visible.

I have spent years watching teams build software that works and software that fails, and the difference is rarely the technology. The difference is almost always whether the team had a shared, explicit model of what they were building and why.

What this book is about
This is not a book about tools, frameworks, or languages. It is a book about the decisions that precede all of those — and the discipline of writing them down.

The apprenticeship problem

In a traditional craft, knowledge passes through apprenticeship. The master does the work; the apprentice watches, asks questions, gradually earns the right to do more. The model is transferred through proximity and time.

Software does not work this way — and cannot, at scale. A team of fifty engineers cannot all apprentice under the three who know why the system is the way it is. The knowledge must be written.

The goal of documentation is not to record what was built. It is to transfer the model that made it possible to build it.

This is the distinction that most documentation fails to make. A README that says what a service does is not governance. A document that says why the service was built this way, what constraints it must satisfy, and what must be true before changing it — that is governance.
